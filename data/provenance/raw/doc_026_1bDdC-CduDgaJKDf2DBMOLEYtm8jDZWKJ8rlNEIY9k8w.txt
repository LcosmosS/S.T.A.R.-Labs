1. Introduction
1.1 Background
The Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer (BSD) conjecture is a fundamental problem in number theory, linking the rank of an elliptic curve (the number of independent rational points) to the behavior of its associated L-function at s = 1. The conjecture posits that the order of the zero of the L-function at this point equals the rank of the curve, providing a deep connection between algebraic geometry and analytic number theory.
In cosmology, star formation rates (SFR) in galaxies are influenced by multiple physical factors, such as stellar mass, redshift, gas density, and environmental effects. Predicting SFR accurately requires models that can capture these complex, multidimensional relationships. Traditional approaches often rely on empirical or semi-empirical formulas, but these can lack a rigorous mathematical foundation.
1.2 Hypothesis
We hypothesize that the distribution of galaxy properties (e.g., mass, redshift, SFR) can be analogized to points on an elliptic curve, with the "rank" representing the number of independent physical drivers of SFR. By constructing a cosmological L-function and analyzing its behavior at s = 1, we can estimate this rank and derive a mathematically grounded SFR prediction formula. This approach aims to replace arbitrary, data-driven coefficients with expressions rooted in first principles, enhancing both the accuracy and interpretability of SFR models.

To mirror the BSD L-function, we defined a cosmological L-function L(s) = Σ a_n n^-s, where:
* a_n represents the count of galaxies within 3D bins of logmass, z, and petrorad_r.
* n is a 1D index of the bins.
This L-function encapsulates the statistical distribution of galaxy properties, analogous to how the BSD L-function encodes the distribution of rational points on an elliptic curve.
2.3 Estimating the Rank
We analyzed the L-function’s behavior near s = 1 by approximating its derivative. If the derivative dL/ds |_s=1 is non-zero, we estimate a first-order zero (rank 1), suggesting one dominant physical driver of SFR. If the derivative is close to zero, we estimate a higher-order zero (rank ≥ 2), indicating multiple independent drivers.
