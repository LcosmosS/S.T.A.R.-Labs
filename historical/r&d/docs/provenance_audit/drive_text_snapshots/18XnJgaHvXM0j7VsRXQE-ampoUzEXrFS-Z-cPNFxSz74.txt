Abstract:
This paper explores a potential connection between the Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer (BSD) conjecture, a central problem in number theory, and cosmology. We investigate this relationship through the distribution of galaxy stellar masses, utilizing data from the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS). The core idea is to define a cosmological analogue to the L-function appearing in the BSD conjecture, and to examine the behavior of its normalization constant. 1. Introduction
The Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer (BSD) conjecture is a central problem in number theory. It relates the arithmetic properties of an elliptic curve (an algebraic equation defining a curve) to the behavior of its L-function, a complex function associated with the curve. Specifically, the BSD conjecture states that the rank of the elliptic curve (a measure of the number of independent rational points on the curve) is equal to the order of the zero of the L-function at s = 1.  Moreover, the conjecture predicts a precise formula for the leading coefficient of the Taylor expansion of the L-function at s = 1, involving several important arithmetic invariants of the elliptic curve.
In this paper, we draw inspiration from the BSD conjecture to explore analogous concepts in cosmology. We investigate whether the distribution of galaxy stellar masses, as observed in large-scale galaxy surveys, can provide insights into the structure and evolution of the universe, in a way that is mathematically analogous to the BSD conjecture. We define a "cosmological L-function" based on galaxy stellar masses and examine its properties.
2. Research Question
Can we establish a meaningful connection between the Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer (BSD) conjecture and cosmology through the distribution of galaxy stellar masses? Further, can we use this framework to construct a 3D topographical map of the universe that measures distances in light-years, represents density as topography, references the Virgo cluster for scale, and ensures mapped objects maintain their natural size, shape, curvature, and distances without discrepancies?
3. Hypothesis
For large samples of galaxies, the normalization constant K, derived from a cosmological L-function Lcosmo(s) constructed using SDSS DR17 stellar mass data, approaches 1, suggesting an analogy to the normalization behavior in the BSD conjecture. This relationship can be visualized through a 3D map of the universe, where galaxy density is represented as topography, distances are measured in light-years, and the Virgo cluster serves as a reference for scale, preserving the true physical properties of cosmic structures.
4. Reasoning
The BSD conjecture posits a profound link between the behavior of an elliptic curve's L-function at s = 1 and the curve's algebraic rank, with the normalization of the L-function reflecting underlying arithmetic structures (Birch & Swinnerton-Dyer, 1965). This suggests that the L-function carries important information about the underlying mathematical object (the elliptic curve).
By analogy, we hypothesize that a suitably defined "cosmological L-function" can capture essential information about the distribution of galaxies in the universe. The normalization constant K, derived from this L-function, is expected to reflect fundamental properties of this distribution. If the distribution of galaxy stellar masses exhibits certain regularities (e.g., a log-normal distribution), then K should approach 1. This would suggest a deep connection between the mathematical structure of the galaxy distribution and the behavior of the cosmological L-function, mirroring the relationship between the arithmetic of elliptic curves and their L-functions in the BSD conjecture. The 3D map then serves as a visualization of this structure.
