Introduction 


What would a world map look like if there was no mapped curvature of the earth included, where countries were drawn according to their actual measurable size?


Creating a world map without accounting for the Earth's curvature and drawing countries according to their actual measurable size (i.e., their true surface area) is a fascinating thought experiment. 


However, it’s also a complex challenge because the Earth is a three-dimensional sphere (or more precisely, an oblate spheroid), and any attempt to represent it on a flat, two-dimensional surface inherently introduces some form of distortion. 


Let’s break this down step-by-step to understand what such a map might look like and the implications of ignoring curvature while prioritizing actual measurable size.
________________


1. Understanding the Problem: Curvature and Map Projections


The Earth's curvature means that its surface cannot be perfectly flattened without distorting either shapes, areas, distances, or angles. Traditional map projections, like the Mercator projection, attempt to balance these distortions for specific purposes:


* Mercator Projection: Preserves angles for navigation but severely distorts areas, especially near the poles (e.g., Greenland appears as large as Africa, despite Africa being 14 times larger in reality—30.4 million km² vs. 2.2 million km² for Greenland).
* Equal-Area Projections: Like the Mollweide or Sinusoidal projections, preserve area but distort shapes and angles, especially at the edges of the map.
If we ignore the Earth's curvature entirely and draw countries according to their actual measurable size (surface area), we’re essentially pretending the Earth is a flat plane and scaling each country’s representation to match its real area. 


This approach aligns with an equal-area projection but with the added constraint of ignoring the spherical geometry of the Earth, which introduces its own set of challenges.
________________


2. What Would Such a Map Look Like?
To create a map where countries are drawn according to their actual measurable size (area) while ignoring curvature, we would:


* Use the real surface area of each country or continent, measured in square kilometers or square miles.
* Arrange these landmasses on a flat plane without considering the spherical relationships (like the way longitude lines converge at the poles or how the equator divides the globe).
Here’s what this might look like:


a) Accurate Areas, But Distorted Shapes and Positions


* Africa: With an area of 30.4 million km², Africa would be massive—much larger than it appears on a Mercator map. It would dominate the map, potentially taking up a central position. In reality, Africa is large enough to fit the United States (9.8 million km²), China (9.6 million km²), India (3.3 million km²), and most of Europe (10.2 million km²) inside it with room to spare.
* Greenland: On a Mercator map, Greenland looks comparable to Africa, but its actual area is only 2.2 million km². On this new map, Greenland would shrink dramatically to about 1/14th the size of Africa.
* North America and Europe: These regions, often exaggerated in size on Mercator maps due to their northern latitudes, would appear smaller. For example, Canada (9.98 million km²) and Russia (17.1 million km²) would still be large but would no longer dominate the map as they do in Mercator projections, where they appear to take up 25% of the Earth’s surface despite occupying only 5% in reality.
* Countries Near the Equator: Nations like Brazil (8.5 million km²) or Kenya (0.58 million km²) would appear larger than they do on a Mercator map, where equatorial regions are compressed.
b) Ignoring Curvature: A Flat Earth Assumption


By ignoring the Earth’s curvature, we’re essentially treating the Earth as if it were a flat plane to begin with. This means:


* No Convergence at the Poles: On a spherical Earth, longitude lines converge at the North and South Poles, which is why polar regions get stretched in many projections. In this flat map, there’s no convergence, so polar regions like Antarctica (14 million km²) would be represented as a flat, sprawling landmass without the distortion caused by trying to "unwrap" a sphere.
* Distorted Relative Positions: The spherical relationships between continents would be lost. For example, the distance between North America and Asia across the Bering Strait is relatively small on a globe because of the curvature over the Arctic. On a flat map ignoring curvature, this relationship would be harder to represent accurately, and continents might be arranged in a more arbitrary way to fit the plane.
* No Curved Flight Paths: Flight paths on a globe are curved due to the Earth’s shape (e.g., a flight from New York to Tokyo arcs over the Arctic). On this flat map, such paths would be straight lines, which would misrepresent real-world distances and travel routes.
c) Visual Characteristics


* Rectangular or Grid-Like Layout: Without curvature, the map might resemble a grid where continents are placed based on their actual area but without regard for their spherical arrangement. For example, you might place Africa in the center, then fit North America, South America, Asia, and Australia around it, scaling each to their true area.
* Shape Distortion: To maintain accurate areas on a flat plane, the shapes of countries would likely be distorted. For instance, Russia, which spans a wide range of longitudes across the Northern Hemisphere, might appear squashed or stretched in odd ways to fit its 17.1 million km² into a flat layout without considering how it wraps around the globe.
* No Poles as Points: On a globe, the North and South Poles are single points where longitude lines converge. On this flat map, the polar regions would be spread out as flat areas, potentially making Antarctica look like a long, thin strip rather than a circular continent centered on the South Pole.

While this map would accurately represent the measurable size (area) of countries, it would introduce other distortions:

Ignoring curvature might place Australia (7.7 million km²) far from Asia (44.6 million km²) in a way that doesn’t reflect their real-world proximity across the Indian Ocean.

* Cultural Misconceptions: As noted in the web results, Mercator maps have historically skewed perceptions of size, making Europe and North America appear more dominant. This new map would correct those area distortions but might create new misconceptions by misrepresenting distances and spatial relationships.

This hypothetical map would resemble an equal-area projection like the Mollweide or Gall-Peters projection, which prioritize accurate areas over shapes or angles. 


However, those projections still account for the Earth’s curvature in their design:


* Gall-Peters Projection: Stretches shapes vertically near the poles and compresses them near the equator to maintain area accuracy. Africa looks tall and narrow, but its area is correct.
* Mollweide Projection: Curves the edges of continents to mimic the globe’s shape while preserving area, but this still reflects the Earth’s curvature.
The key difference in this scenario is to ignore curvature. This would make the map look more like a simple tiling of landmasses, where each country is a flat, area-accurate shape, but their arrangement and shapes would be even more distorted than in a typical equal-area projection.

1. Conceptual Overview: A 3D Topographical Map Without Curvature
A traditional 3D topographical map of the Earth, such as a globe or a 3D-printed model, represents the planet as a sphere (or oblate spheroid) with raised features to show elevation. 


For example:


* Mountains like the Himalayas or the Andes are raised to reflect their height above sea level.
* Ocean basins are lower, and flat plains are at an intermediate height.
In this scenario, we’re ignoring the Earth’s curvature, meaning treating the Earth as a flat plane in the x-y dimensions (latitude and longitude), while still using the z-dimension (height) to represent topography. 


Additionally, we’re ensuring that each country or landmass is scaled to its actual measurable surface area in the x-y plane. 


Here’s how this would work:


* Flat Base Plane: The map’s base (the x-y plane) is completely flat, as if the Earth were a flat surface rather than a sphere. * Accurate Areas: Each country’s footprint on the flat x-y plane is proportional to its actual surface area, correcting for distortions seen in maps like the Mercator projection (where Greenland appears as large as Africa despite being 14 times smaller).

What about a map where countries remained the same size and degree of curvature, but the globe was so large that if zoomed in it would appear non spherical; does the answer lie within The Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture?

What is the Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer Conjecture?
The Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture (often abbreviated as BSD) deals with elliptic curves, which are mathematical objects defined by equations of the form y^2 = x^3 + ax + b, where ( a ) and ( b ) are constants, and the curve has no singular points (i.e., the discriminant 4a^3 + 27b^2 \neq 0). 


These curves have a rich structure and are central to many areas of mathematics, including number theory and cryptography.


The conjecture describes the relationship between two seemingly different properties of an elliptic curve defined over the rational numbers (\mathbb{Q}):


1. The rank of the elliptic curve: This is the number of independent rational points (solutions to the curve’s equation where both ( x ) and ( y ) are rational numbers) that generate the curve’s group of rational points under a certain operation (called the Mordell-Weil group). The rank is a measure of how many “free” rational solutions exist.
2. The behavior of the curve’s L-function at a specific point: The L-function of an elliptic curve is a complex function that encodes information about the curve’s arithmetic properties, particularly how its points behave modulo prime numbers.
The conjecture, named after Bryan Birch and Peter Swinnerton-Dyer, posits a precise connection between these two quantities. Specifically:


* The L-function of an elliptic curve ( E ), denoted ( L(E, s) ), is an analytic object (a function of a complex variable ( s )) that can be constructed from the curve.
* The conjecture states that the order of the zero of ( L(E, s) ) at s = 1 (called the analytic rank) is equal to the rank of the elliptic curve’s Mordell-Weil group (the algebraic rank).
* Furthermore, the leading term of the Taylor expansion of ( L(E, s) ) at s = 1 is related to other arithmetic invariants of the curve, such as the size of the Tate-Shafarevich group (a mysterious group that measures the failure of the Hasse principle), the regulator (a measure of the “size” of the rational points), and various local factors.
In simpler terms, the conjecture predicts that the number of rational solutions to an elliptic curve’s equation can be determined by studying the behavior of its L-function at s = 1. 


If the L-function vanishes to order ( r ) at s = 1, then the rank of the elliptic curve should be ( r ).
________________


Historical Context and Development
The conjecture originated in the 1960s when Peter Swinnerton-Dyer, using the EDSAC-2 computer at the University of Cambridge, computed the number of points on elliptic curves modulo primes ( p ) (denoted N_p) for many primes. 


Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer analyzed this data and noticed a pattern: the number N_p seemed to grow in a way that depended on the rank of the curve. They formulated their conjecture in 1965, as noted in the web result, proposing that:


\prod_{p \leq X} \frac{N_p}{p} \sim C (\log X)^r


where ( r ) is the rank of the curve, ( X ) is a large bound on the primes, and ( C ) is a constant. 


This asymptotic behavior suggested a deep link between the arithmetic of the curve (its rank) and its analytic properties (encoded in the L-function).
________________


Key Components of the Conjecture
The Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture has two main parts:


1. The Rank Prediction:
   * The order of the zero of ( L(E, s) ) at s = 1 equals the rank of the elliptic curve’s Mordell-Weil group.
   * Mathematically: If the rank of E(\mathbb{Q}) is ( r ), then ( L(E, s) ) has a zero of order ( r ) at s = 1.
2. The Refined Conjecture:
   * The leading term of the Taylor expansion of ( L(E, s) ) at s = 1 is given by a formula involving several arithmetic invariants:
   * L(E, s) \sim c (s-1)^r \quad \text{as} \quad s \to 1,
   * where ( c ) is a constant that depends on:
      * The regulator ( R ), which measures the “volume” of the rational points.
      * The order of the Tate-Shafarevich group \text{Sha}(E), denoted |\text{Sha}|, which captures obstructions to finding rational points.
      * The Tamagawa numbers, which are local factors at each prime where the curve has bad reduction.
      * The discriminant of the curve and the real period (related to the curve’s geometry).
The full statement of the conjecture is:


\lim_{s \to 1} \frac{L(E, s)}{(s-1)^r} = \frac{|\text{Sha}| \cdot R \cdot \prod_p c_p}{\# E_{\text{tors}}^2},


where E_{\text{tors}} is the torsion subgroup of the Mordell-Weil group, and \prod_p c_p is the product of the Tamagawa numbers.
________________


Significance and Implications
The Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture is significant for several reasons:


* Connection Between Analysis and Arithmetic: It bridges analytic number theory (via the L-function) and algebraic number theory (via the rank of the elliptic curve), providing a profound link between two different mathematical domains.
* Applications to Diophantine Equations: Elliptic curves are tied to Diophantine equations (equations where we seek integer or rational solutions). The conjecture has implications for problems like finding congruent numbers (numbers that are the area of a right triangle with rational side lengths). As the web result mentions, the BSD conjecture implies that an odd square-free integer ( n ) is a congruent number if and only if the number of integer solutions to 2x^2 + y^2 + 8z^2 = n is twice the number of solutions to 2x^2 + y^2 + 32z^2 = n.
* Relation to Other Conjectures: The BSD conjecture is analogous to the behavior of the Riemann zeta function for number fields. For example, the Riemann zeta function \zeta(s) has a simple pole at s = 1, and its residue is related to the class number of the field. The BSD conjecture extends this idea to elliptic curves, where the L-function’s behavior at s = 1 reflects the curve’s arithmetic properties.

Progress and Partial Results
The conjecture remains open, but significant progress has been made, as highlighted in the web result:


* Numerical Evidence: Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer’s original work in the 1960s provided strong numerical evidence for the conjecture by computing N_p for many elliptic curves and observing the predicted asymptotic behavior.
* Special Cases:
   * Rank 0 and 1: The conjecture has been proven for elliptic curves with rank 0 or 1 in many cases. For example, Victor Kolyvagin (1989) showed that if the analytic rank of an elliptic curve over \mathbb{Q} is 0 or 1, then the algebraic rank matches it, and the Tate-Shafarevich group is finite.
   * Complex Multiplication: Karl Rubin (1991) proved that for elliptic curves over imaginary quadratic fields with complex multiplication, if L(E, 1) \neq 0, then the ( p )-part of the Tate-Shafarevich group has the order predicted by BSD for all primes p > 7.
* Average Results: Bhargava and Shankar (2015) showed that the average rank of elliptic curves over \mathbb{Q} is bounded above by 7/6. Combined with results like the p-parity theorem (Nekovář, 2009; Dokchitser & Dokchitser, 2010) and the main conjecture of Iwasawa theory for \text{GL}(2) (Skinner & Urban, 2014), this implies that a positive proportion of elliptic curves over \mathbb{Q} have analytic rank 0, and thus satisfy the BSD conjecture (via Kolyvagin’s work).
* Modular Curves: The modularity theorem (proven by Wiles and Taylor-Wiles for semistable elliptic curves, and later extended by Breuil-Conrad-Diamond-Taylor) ensures that every elliptic curve over \mathbb{Q} is modular, meaning its L-function can be studied via modular forms. This has been crucial for testing and proving special cases of BSD.

Challenges and Open Questions
The Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture is notoriously difficult for several reasons:


* Tate-Shafarevich Group: The group \text{Sha}(E) is conjectured to be finite, but this is not proven in general. Its size is a key part of the refined conjecture, but computing or bounding \text{Sha} is extremely challenging.
* Higher Ranks: While the conjecture is well-understood for ranks 0 and 1, cases with higher ranks (e.g., r \geq 2) are much harder. There are elliptic curves with arbitrarily large ranks (the current record is a curve with rank at least 28, constructed by Elkies in 2006), but verifying BSD for these cases is an open problem.
* Analytic Difficulties: Computing the L-function and its behavior at s = 1 requires sophisticated analytic tools, especially for curves with bad reduction at certain primes.
