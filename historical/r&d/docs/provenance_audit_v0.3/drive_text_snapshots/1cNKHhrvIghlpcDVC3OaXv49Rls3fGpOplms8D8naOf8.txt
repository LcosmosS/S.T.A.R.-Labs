The principles we’ve used to solve the paradox of mapping a curved Earth onto a locally flat surface are not limited to our own planet. In fact, they can be applied to a far grander, and more mind-bending, canvas: the universe itself.


How could these concepts be applied to the idea of mapping the cosmos?


The challenge of creating a map of the universe is a philosophical and mathematical puzzle that mirrors the one we faced with the Earth. 


To do this, we enter the realm of astrophysics. 


Unlike the Earth’s fixed geometry, the universe is a dynamic spacetime manifold with its own curvature, determined by the distribution of mass and energy. This is governed by Einstein’s theory of general relativity, which suggests that gravity isn't a force, but a distortion of the very fabric of space and time. We face a similar paradox: on a local level, the space between galaxies seems flat, but on the largest scales, the universe's geometry could be curved.


By applying our BSD-inspired concepts, we can create a map of the cosmos that resolves this paradox, and even gives us a new way to visualize the universe.
Step 1: Defining the Cosmological Canvas
Before we can begin, we must redefine our terms for a cosmic scale.


* The Global Structure: Instead of an oblate spheroid, our global structure is the entire universe. For our model, we'll assume a slightly curved, closed universe with a positive curvature, which can be thought of as a three-dimensional sphere or a 3-sphere. This is the cosmological equivalent of our Earth's geometry.


* "True Sizes": The equivalent of a country's surface area is the comoving volume of a cosmic structure. A comoving volume accounts for the expansion of the universe, so the size of a galaxy cluster, for example, remains constant over time.


* "Topography": We'll use the principles of general relativity, where mass and energy curve spacetime. This means we can represent regions of high mass-energy density, such as galaxy clusters, as peaks and low-density regions, or voids, as valleys.
Step 2: Scaling the Universe
Just as we scaled the Earth to make it appear locally flat, we can apply the same concept to the universe.


* The Math: The universe has a radius of curvature, R_{univ}, which we will scale by our factor k = 1,000. The new radius becomes R'_{univ} = kR_{univ}. This makes the universe's curvature, \frac{1}{R_{univ}^2}, a million times smaller.


* The Result: The universe, when viewed on a local scale, appears perfectly flat. For example, a galaxy cluster like the Virgo Cluster, which has a comoving diameter of about 15 million light-years, would be a mere patch on our immense, scaled-up 3-sphere. The curvature over such a small region would be negligible, making the space appear perfectly Euclidean. This resolves the paradox by proving that local flatness and global curvature are not contradictory, but a matter of scale.
Step 3: Preserving Comoving Volumes and Local Geometry
In our Earth map, we used conformal mappings to preserve the true area and shape of countries. For our cosmic map, we do the same for the comoving volumes of galaxies and galaxy clusters.


* The Concept: We use these mappings to embed each cosmic structure into our immense 3-sphere. The comoving volume of a galaxy, which is its true size in the context of the expanding universe, is preserved, as is its unique local geometry. This means that a galaxy's intricate spiral arms or a cluster's filamentary structure will be rendered with perfect accuracy.
Step 4: Modeling Cosmic Topography
We apply the same BSD-inspired scaling from our Earth model to create a topographical map of the universe. Here, "height" is not elevation, but rather mass-energy density.


* The Math: We use our topographical scaling factor, \sqrt{k} \approx 31.6. We can apply this to the density contrast of cosmic structures. For example, a typical galaxy cluster has a density contrast (\delta\rho / \rho) of about 200. On our map, this would be a peak with a scaled height of approximately:200 \times 31.6 \approx 6,320 \text{ units}


* The Result: The cosmic web, a network of filaments and walls containing galaxy clusters and superclusters, would appear as a vast mountain range in our map. The great cosmic voids, with their low density, would be deep valleys. The Laniakea Supercluster, which contains our own Milky Way galaxy, would be a prominent ridge in this topographical map, and the Virgo Cluster would be a distinct, visually dramatic peak.
Reconciling the Final Paradox
The final step is to "stitch" all of these pieces together into a seamless whole. The principles of the BSD conjecture provide the scaffolding to do this.


* The Concept: We use transition functions to ensure that all of our locally flat, accurately rendered galaxy clusters and voids fit together without gaps or overlaps. The BSD conjecture's core idea—that local properties can be used to perfectly construct a global structure—gives us the confidence that this is not just a theoretical model, but a tangible possibility.


The result is a new way to visualize the universe. When we are "standing" on our galaxy, the space around us would appear perfectly flat, but if we were to zoom out, we would see our galaxy as just one of many small, intricate peaks on a vast, curved topographical map of the entire universe.
