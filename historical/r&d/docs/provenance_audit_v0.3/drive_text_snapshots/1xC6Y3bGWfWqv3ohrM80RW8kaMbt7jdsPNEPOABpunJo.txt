Recap: What is the Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer Conjecture?
The Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer (BSD) conjecture is one of the most profound open problems in mathematics, specifically in the field of number theory, and it’s one of the seven Clay Mathematics Institute Millennium Prize Problems. 


Proving it in full generality would be a monumental achievement, and as of March 2025, it remains unproven, though significant progress has been made. 


Let’s walk through what the conjecture states, the progress made so far, and why proving it fully is beyond current mathematical capabilities, and also outline the key ideas and partial results to get a sense of the landscape.


The BSD conjecture relates to elliptic curves, which are mathematical objects defined by equations of the form y^2 = x^3 + ax + b, where ( a ) and ( b ) are constants, and the curve is non-singular (the discriminant \Delta = -16(4a^3 + 27b^2) \neq 0). 


Elliptic curves have a group structure, meaning their points (solutions to the equation) can be added together in a specific way. 


The conjecture connects the arithmetic of an elliptic curve ( E ) over a number field (typically the rationals \mathbb{Q}) to the analytic behavior of its L-function.


Key Components:

1. The Mordell-Weil Group: The group of rational points E(\mathbb{Q}) on an elliptic curve ( E ) over \mathbb{Q} is finitely generated (Mordell-Weil theorem), so it can be written as:
2. E(\mathbb{Q}) \cong \mathbb{Z}^r \oplus T,
3. where ( T ) is a finite torsion subgroup, and ( r ) is the rank of the curve (the number of independent infinite-order points).
4. The L-function: The L-function ( L(E, s) ) of an elliptic curve ( E ) is defined as an infinite product over all prime numbers ( p ):
5. L(E, s) = \prod_p L_p(E, s),
6. where L_p(E, s) depends on the number of points on ( E ) over the finite field \mathbb{F}_p. For a prime ( p ) of good reduction (where ( E ) remains non-singular modulo ( p )), the local factor is:
7. L_p(E, s) = \left( 1 - a_p p^{-s} + p^{1-2s} \right)^{-1},
8. with a_p = p + 1 - |E(\mathbb{F}_p)|, and |E(\mathbb{F}_p)| being the number of points on ( E ) modulo ( p ). The L-function converges for \text{Re}(s) > 3/2, but it can be analytically continued to the entire complex plane via the modularity theorem (proven by Wiles et al. as part of the proof of Fermat’s Last Theorem).
9. The BSD Conjecture:
   * Weak BSD: The rank ( r ) of E(\mathbb{Q}) equals the order of the zero of ( L(E, s) ) at s = 1. That is, if we write the Taylor expansion of ( L(E, s) ) around s = 1:
   * L(E, s) \sim c (s-1)^r + \text{higher-order terms},
   * then the conjecture states that the analytic rank (the order of the zero, i.e., the smallest ( k ) such that the ( k )-th derivative L^{(k)}(E, 1) \neq 0) equals the algebraic rank ( r ).
   * Strong BSD: If the order of the zero at s = 1 is ( r ), then the leading coefficient of the Taylor expansion is given by a precise formula involving arithmetic invariants of ( E ):
   * \lim_{s \to 1} \frac{L(E, s)}{(s-1)^r} = \frac{\Omega \cdot \text{Reg}(E) \cdot |\text{Sha}(E)| \cdot \prod_p c_p}{|E(\mathbb{Q})_{\text{tors}}|^2},
   * where:
      * \Omega is the real period of ( E ) (related to the integral of a differential form over the curve),
      * \text{Reg}(E) is the regulator (a determinant of a height pairing on the free part of E(\mathbb{Q})),
      * |\text{Sha}(E)| is the order of the Tate-Shafarevich group (a mysterious group measuring the failure of the local-to-global principle for ( E )),
      * \prod_p c_p is the product of Tamagawa numbers (local factors at each prime ( p )),
      * |E(\mathbb{Q})_{\text{tors}}| is the order of the torsion subgroup of E(\mathbb{Q}).
The conjecture essentially says that the analytic behavior of ( L(E, s) ) at s = 1 encodes deep arithmetic information about ( E ).

Why Proving BSD is Hard
The BSD conjecture is difficult for several reasons:


1. The Tate-Shafarevich Group: The group \text{Sha}(E) is notoriously hard to compute. It’s conjectured to be finite, but this is not proven in general. If \text{Sha}(E) were infinite, the strong BSD formula would need reinterpretation.
2. Analytic and Algebraic Connection: Relating the analytic rank (from the L-function) to the algebraic rank (from the Mordell-Weil group) requires deep insights into both number theory and complex analysis.
3. General Case: The conjecture applies to all elliptic curves over \mathbb{Q}, and elliptic curves can have vastly different behaviors depending on their rank, conductor, and other invariants.

Progress on BSD
While the full conjecture remains open, significant progress has been made:


1. Rank 0 and 1 Cases:
   * In the 1980s, Gross-Zagier and Kolyvagin proved the weak BSD for elliptic curves with analytic rank \leq 1. Specifically:
      * Gross-Zagier (1986) showed that if L(E, 1) \neq 0, then the rank of E(\mathbb{Q}) is 0, and if L'(E, 1) \neq 0, then the rank is 1.
      * Kolyvagin (1989) proved the converse: if the rank is 0 or 1, then the analytic rank matches (i.e., L(E, 1) \neq 0 or L'(E, 1) \neq 0).
   * These results also imply that \text{Sha}(E) is finite in these cases.
2. Positive Proportion of Curves:
   * Bhargava, Shankar, and others (around 2015–2020) showed that a positive proportion of elliptic curves over \mathbb{Q}, when ordered by height, satisfy the weak BSD. They used techniques from arithmetic statistics and the geometry of numbers to study the distribution of ranks and L-values.
   * For example, Bhargava and Shankar proved that at least 66% of elliptic curves have rank 0 or 1, and for these curves, the weak BSD holds (due to Gross-Zagier and Kolyvagin).
3. p-adic BSD:
   * There’s a p-adic version of BSD, which relates the p-adic L-function of an elliptic curve to its Selmer group. Significant progress has been made here, notably by Kato, Skinner, and Urban, who proved one direction of the p-adic BSD for many curves (if the rank is less than the order of the zero of the p-adic L-function, then the Selmer group has the expected rank).
4. Modularity and L-functions:
   * The modularity theorem (proven by Wiles, Taylor, and others in the 1990s) ensures that every elliptic curve over \mathbb{Q} has a well-defined L-function that can be analytically continued. This was a crucial step, as BSD relies on the existence and properties of ( L(E, s) ).
5. Numerical Evidence:
   * The BSD conjecture has been tested extensively for specific elliptic curves. For example, the curve y^2 = x^3 - x (conductor 37) has rank 1, and its L-function has a simple zero at s = 1, matching the weak BSD. The strong BSD has also been verified numerically for many curves, though the Tate-Shafarevich group is often hard to compute.

Proving BSD?
Proving the BSD conjecture in full generality would require: 


1. A method to compute or bound \text{Sha}(E) for all elliptic curves, which is an open problem.
2. A general way to relate the analytic rank to the algebraic rank for all ranks ( r ), not just r \leq 1.
3. A proof of the strong BSD formula, which involves understanding the regulator, Tamagawa numbers, and other invariants in all cases.
The partial results (e.g., Gross-Zagier, Kolyvagin, Bhargava-Shankar) rely on advanced techniques like Heegner points, Euler systems, and arithmetic statistics—tools that are highly specialized and not fully generalizable yet. 


Moreover, the conjecture’s reliance on \text{Sha}(E) makes it particularly challenging, as we lack a general method to prove its finiteness or compute its order.
A Sketch of a Partial Approach (Rank 0 Case)
To get a sense of how one might approach BSD, let’s outline the proof for the rank 0 case, which is known (thanks to Gross-Zagier and Kolyvagin). 


Suppose ( E ) is an elliptic curve over \mathbb{Q} with rank 0, meaning E(\mathbb{Q}) is finite (just the torsion subgroup).


1. Analytic Side: Compute ( L(E, 1) ). If L(E, 1) \neq 0, the analytic rank is 0 (no zero at s = 1).
2. Algebraic Side: Since the rank is 0, E(\mathbb{Q}) has no infinite-order points. We need to show this matches the analytic rank.
3. Gross-Zagier Formula: Gross-Zagier constructed a Heegner point ( P ) on ( E ) (using a modular parametrization and a point on a related modular curve). They showed that the height of ( P ) is related to ( L'(E, 1) ). If L(E, 1) \neq 0, the height of ( P ) is 0, implying ( P ) is a torsion point, so the rank is 0.
4. Kolyvagin’s Euler System: Kolyvagin used an Euler system of Heegner points to bound the Selmer group of ( E ), showing that if L(E, 1) \neq 0, the Selmer group is finite, confirming the rank is 0.
This proves the weak BSD for rank 0: if L(E, 1) \neq 0, then the rank is 0, and vice versa. 


The strong BSD in this case involves computing ( L(E, 1) ) and verifying the formula, which has been done for many curves but not in full generality.
