The maps we have been discussing, with their paradoxical blend of local flatness and global curvature, are built on a new set of rules. This leads to a crucial question: would a map like this also keep distances true? 
The answer is both yes and no, depending on the scale at which we measure.
The Earth Model: The Distance from the U.S. to Ireland
Let's apply our theory to the classic example of travel between the United States and Ireland. On a traditional flat map, the distance is a straight line, but on a globe, the shortest path is a great circle route.


* The True Distance: The actual, shortest distance between any two points on a sphere is along what is known as a great circle route. A great circle is the largest possible circle that can be drawn on the surface of a sphere. To visualize this, imagine slicing a perfectly round orange directly through its center. The rind where the knife cut is a great circle. Any other slice that doesn't go through the center creates a smaller circle, known as a small circle.


Navigators use great circles because they represent the shortest distance between two points on the globe, much like a straight line is the shortest distance on a flat plane. For example, the great circle distance between New York City and Dublin is approximately 5,150 km. This is why a commercial flight between these two cities appears to follow a curved path over the Atlantic when viewed on a flat map—the plane is flying the straightest possible route over a curved surface.


* The Global Paradox: Our map scales the Earth's radius by a factor of k = 1,000. This means that the entire globe is now a thousand times larger. The great circle distance between the two cities is also stretched by this factor, becoming 5,150,000 km. In this sense, the map does not keep global distances true, as they are a thousand times greater than in reality.


* The Local Solution: The map resolves this paradox through conformal mappings, which preserve local geometry. While the entire globe is scaled up, the ruler we use to measure on our local, flat patch is also scaled down by the same factor. This means that a measurement on our local map gives us the true, 5,150 km distance, making the map's local representation perfectly accurate.


So, in the Earth model, the map keeps distances true on a local scale but not a global one. It is a tool for navigating within a country or a continent, but a poor guide for a world-circling journey.
The Cosmos Model: The Distance from Earth to Mars
The challenge of mapping the cosmos is different because of a key concept in astrophysics: comoving distance. This accounts for the expansion of the universe itself.


* The True Distance: The average distance from Earth to Mars is approximately 78 million km, which light travels in 4.33 minutes. For a far more distant object like the Virgo Cluster, the comoving distance is 54 million light-years, which corresponds to a light-travel time of 54 million years. This is the true, cosmological distance we want to preserve.


* The Local Paradox: Our map is locally flat, which means the geometry for navigating within the solar system is perfectly Euclidean. The distance between Earth and Mars is so small compared to the universe's overall curvature that the space between them is already flat, making the map's local representation perfectly accurate without any adjustment. Our map simply formalizes this truth.


* The Global Solution: This is where our cosmological map excels. Unlike the Earth model, the universe's comoving coordinates are invariant under the large-scale scaling of our map. This means that the comoving distance to the Virgo Cluster (54 million light-years) is preserved perfectly, both on the local, flat patch and on the globally curved 3-sphere of the universe.


So, for our cosmological map, the answer is a resounding yes: the map keeps distances true, both locally and globally. It provides an elegant visualization of the universe's structure that aligns perfectly with the way astrophysicists measure cosmic distances.
Conclusion: A Tale of Two Maps
Our theory creates two different, but equally powerful, maps. The Earth map, with its great circle distances, provides a system that is locally accurate but globally distorted. The cosmological map, however, with its comoving distances, provides a unique representation that keeps distances true at every scale, from the solar system to the great cosmic voids. This is why a BSD-inspired map provides a new framework for visualizing and navigating the universe.
