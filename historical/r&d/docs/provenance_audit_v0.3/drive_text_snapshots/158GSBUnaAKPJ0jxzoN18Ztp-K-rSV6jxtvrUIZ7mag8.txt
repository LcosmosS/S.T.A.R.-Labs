1. Introduction


1.1 Background


   The BSD conjecture, a central unsolved problem in number theory, posits a deep connection between elliptic curves and L-functions. 


   It states that the rank of an elliptic curve (the number of independent rational points) is equal to the order of the zero of its associated L-function at s = 1.


  This conjecture has inspired applications far beyond pure mathematics.


   In astrophysics, the star formation rate (SFR) is a crucial parameter for understanding galaxy evolution. 


   SFR is influenced by factors such as stellar mass, redshift, gas density, metallicity, and environmental effects. 


   Traditional SFR models range from empirical relations (e.g., the Kennicutt-Schmidt law) to complex simulations (e.g., IllustrisTNG), each with limitations in scope and predictive power.




   1.2 Motivation and Objectives


   This study aims to develop a mathematically grounded model for SFR prediction based on the BSD conjecture. Our main objectives are:


  • To establish a theoretical analogy between
   elliptic curves and galaxy distributions.


  • To construct a cosmological L-function relevant
   to SFR dynamics.


  • To integrate this L-function into a predictive
   model and assess its performance using
   observational data.




   1.3 Research Questions and Hypotheses


Addressing the following questions:


  • Can a cosmological L-function be constructed
   such that its mathematical properties reflect
   SFR drivers?


  • Does incorporating this L-function improve
   SFR
   prediction accuracy compared to traditional
   models?


   Our central hypothesis is that the BSD conjecture provides a viable mathematical framework for understanding and modeling SFR.




2. Theoretical Framework


   2.1 The Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer Conjecture:
         A Brief Overview


   Consider an elliptic curve E defined over the rational numbers (Q). 


   The BSD conjecture relates the arithmetic of E to the analytic behavior of its L-function, L(E, s). 


Specifically, the BSD conjecture states:


  • The rank of E, denoted rank(E), equals the
   order of the zero of L(E, s) at s = 1.


  • The leading coefficient of the Taylor expansion
   of L(E, s) around s = 1 encodes additional
   arithmetic data, providing deeper insights into
   the curve's properties.


   This conjecture suggests that complex distributions of rational points can be analyzed via analytic functions, a concept I extend to cosmology.






   2.2 Analogy to Galaxy Distributions


   In “BSD Cosmology”, galaxies are treated as points in a multidimensional parameter space, with coordinates such as logarithmic stellar mass (logmass), redshift (z), declination (dec), and Petrosian radius (petrorad_r). 


   Just as the rank of an elliptic curve reflects the number of independent generators of its rational points, the proposition is that the "rank" of a galaxy distribution reflects the number of independent physical processes driving SFR.
(e.g., gas accretion, stellar feedback).




   2.3 Defining the Cosmological L-Function


   To operationalize this analogy define a cosmological L-function:


   Lcosmo(s) = ∑ a_n n^(-s),


where:


  • n indexes bins in the galaxy parameter space
   (e.g., discretized by mass and redshift).


  • a_n is the number of galaxies in bin n,
   optionally weighted by physical properties like
   SFR or density.


  The behavior of Lcosmo(s) at s = 1 is hypothesized to indicate the number of independent SFR drivers, mirroring the BSD conjecture.






3. Model Development


   3.1 Constructing the L-Function


To operationalize Lcosmo(s):


  • Binning: Discretize galaxy properties into a
   grid. For example, logmass in 0.1 dex intervals
   and z in 0.01 intervals.


  • Coefficients: Set a_n as the galaxy count per
   bin, weighted by observed SFR to emphasize
   star-forming regions.


  • Analytic Continuation: Approximate Lcosmo(s)
   numerically, focusing on its value and
   derivatives at s = 1.


   For simplicity, initially assume a rank-1
scenario (one dominant driver), adjusting the model as data suggests higher complexity.






   3.2 SFR Prediction Formula


The SFR is modeled as:


   SFR = a * logmass + b * z + c * Lcosmo(1) + d *
   environment + e * petrorad_r,


where:


  • logmass and z are primary predictors from
   cosmological literature.


  • Lcosmo(1) introduces the BSD-inspired term.


  • environment (derived from spatial coordinates)
   and petrorad_r account for additional physics.


  • Coefficients a, b, c, d, and e are optimized via
   regression.

  • Preprocess data: Handle missing values,

  • Compute Lcosmo(s) over binned data.

5. Results and Analysis


   5.1 Numerical Evaluation of Lcosmo(s)


   Numerical evaluation suggests Lcosmo(s) has a first-order zero at s = 1 (rank 1), implying one dominant SFR driver (e.g., stellar mass), with secondary factors refining the prediction. 
This aligns with cosmological intuition that mass is a primary driver.






   5.2 Model Performance


The BSD Cosmology model demonstrates the following performance:


  • Average MSE: 0.4266
 (logarithmic units, normalized).


  • Standard Deviation: 0.0185 across folds,
   indicating consistency.


  • Comparison: An earlier model without 
   Lcosmo(1) or environmental terms yielded 
   MSE = 0.6715, demonstrating improvement
   with the inclusion of the BSD-inspired term.






   5.3 Interpretation


The results suggest:


  • The BSD-inspired Lcosmo(1) term reduces
   reliance on empirical coefficients, grounding the
   model in a mathematical framework.


  • The model accurately captures key SFR
   drivers, as evidenced by the low MSE and
   stable cross-validation performance.
